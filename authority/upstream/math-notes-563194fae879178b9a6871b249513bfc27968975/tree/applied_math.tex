\textbf{Disclaimer:} The course ``Applied Mathematics'' is one of the few courses
that does not try to formalize the math behinds its methods.
In such courses, I believe that the best method to succeed is to
learn the language of the course, and practice exercises in order
to get a feeling for the methods.
Indeed, it is not the methods in this course which are not fully formalized,
it's mostly the language.

rather than mathematical definitions.

deemed fit.

\section{Dimensional analysis}

\begin{entry}[Dimension]
  In this course, dimension refers to ``physical units''
  such as $[L]$ (Length), $[T]$ (time) etc.
  For example, the radius of the earth is denoted by $R[L]$,
  where $R$ is a value and $L$ is the dimension of length.
  Dimensions are denoted by captial letters.
\end{entry}

\begin{entry}[Relatively very small]
  We say that $x$ is very small relative to $R$ if it is intuitively a lot
  smaller, and we will not formalize this notion further in this course.
  In such a case, we denote $x \ll R$.
  Sometimes we need to use ``common sense'',
  for example, if $R_1$ is the radius of the earth, and $R_2$
  is the distance from the earth to the sun, then $R_1 \ll R_2$.
\end{entry}

\begin{entry}[(non)dimensional equation]
  A dimensional equation is an equation which considers dimensions.
  For instance, the equation for distance:
  \[
    x[L] = v[L T^{-1}] \times t[T],
  \]
  is a dimensional equation.
  A nondimensional equation is an equation where we do not consider dimensions
  such as Euler's formula:
  \[
    e^{\pi i} + 1 = 0.
  \]
\end{entry}

\begin{entry}[Regular preturbation]
  A regular preturbation is a small variable (possibly constant)
  that has a negligible effect on the solution.
  For example if $100 \ll R$ is a variable, and the actual solution
  is $S(R) = R + 1$ then we could say $1$ is a regular perturbation.
\end{entry}

\begin{entry}[Singular preturbation]
  A singular perturbation is a small variable in the equation which
  affects the solution drastically.
  For example, if our problem is finding the number of zeros of the equation:
  \[
    \epsilon x^2 - 2x + 1 = 0
  \]
  then for small nonzero values of $\epsilon$ the result is $2$,
  but when $\epsilon = 0$ the solution changes drastically to $1$.
  Thus $\epsilon$ is a singular perturbation.
\end{entry}

\begin{entry}[Dimensionally complete equation]
  A dimensionally complete equation is an equation where the RHS and the
  LHS both have the same dimension such as:
  \[
    x[L] = v[L T^{-1}] \times t[T].
  \]
  Equations of the form:
  \[
    x[L] = v[L T^{-1}] \tor
    u(x,t) = x[L] + t[T],
  \]
  are called dimensionally incomplete.
\end{entry}

\begin{entry}[Nondimensionalization]
  Nondimensionalization is the process of changing a dimensional equation
  into a nondimensional equation.
  This is done by normalizing the dimensional variable by some parameter.
  For example if $x[L]$ is the height of a rocket, and $H[L]$ is its
  maximal height, then $\tilde x = \frac{x}{H}$ is dimensionless.
  By plugging $x = \tilde x H$ into the dimensional equation,
  and considering the equation in $\tilde x$ we would get a nondimensional
  equation.
  As you can see, this process is not deterministic
  since different normalizations (also called scalings) give different
  equations.
\end{entry}

\begin{entry}[The $\O(1)$ criterion]
  The $\O(1)$ criterion states that
  in the process of nondimensionalization,
  in order to reach a solution that ``makes sense'',
  we need to have dimensionless variables that are $\O(1)$.
  In this case, a variables $x$ is called $\O(1)$ if $-1 \le x \le 1$
  (we do not use the standard definition of $\O(1)$ for obvious reasons).
\end{entry}

\begin{entry}[Unit of measurement]
  A unit of measurements is a certain arbitrary size that allows us
  to assess dimensions.
  For example, meters, centimeters, my current height,
  are all equally valid units of measurement for the dimension of length.
\end{entry}
